# Research Framework

Use this file when judging quality, valuation, risk, monitoring, or filing clues. Keep each evidence layer separate.

## Business and financial quality

Answer these questions before looking at price or holdings:

- What does the company sell, to whom, and why does demand persist or fade?
- What economic advantage, switching cost, scale, regulation, or weakness actually matters?
- Are earnings converted into cash? Separate recurring cash generation from working-capital swings, one-time gains, stock-based compensation, and accounting changes.
- How does management allocate capital among reinvestment, acquisitions, debt, buybacks, and dividends?
- Can the balance sheet absorb a downturn? Include maturity schedule, covenants, liquidity, customer or supplier concentration, and dilution.

A quality judgment needs a source for every material operating or financial claim. If the latest annual or quarterly filing is unavailable, say the quality assessment is incomplete.

## Valuation

Choose the method from the business model. Free-cash-flow yield, earnings multiple, EV/EBIT, a conservative DCF, or an appropriate historical range may fit. Do not mechanically apply one historical price-to-earnings multiple to every company.

For each conservative, base, and optimistic scenario, state:

- the operating assumptions that drive it;
- the valuation method and comparison or historical context;
- the price and price date;
- why the conservative case could still be wrong.

Express an entry condition or margin-of-safety range, not a false-precision buy price. Historical percentile is background only; do not assume a past multiple returns.

If a required price, share count, estimate, or cash-flow input is missing, show the missing input and stop the numerical conclusion. Do not fill the gap with an undocumented estimate.

## Downside and thesis-breakers

Classify risks as business, financial, valuation, or external. External risk includes regulation, supply chain, litigation, customer or supplier concentration, dilution, and dated events.

For each important risk, state its effect, the observable signal, and the condition that weakens or breaks the thesis. Do not invent an unsupported numeric risk score. Discuss which risks should constrain position size, but never calculate the user's position.

## What requires another look

Name the next event specific to the issuer: earnings and guidance, a maturity or refinancing, a regulatory decision, a major customer or supplier change, a shareholder vote, or a known company action. A vague "monitor the company" is not sufficient.

## Filing clues

Use 13F history only after the fundamental work. Useful clues are:

- repeated holdings by managers whose stated style is relevant;
- a large snapshot-to-snapshot change after checking company actions and duplicate reporting;
- independent managers whose filings corroborate one another;
- the subsequent market move from the quarter-end price, described only as a price path, not as manager profit or cost.

13D and 13G can add beneficial-ownership context. Form 4 can describe insider transactions. Both are generally timelier than 13F, but neither establishes fair value or an appropriate entry point by itself.

Never infer a trade date, full transaction history, or current ownership from two 13F snapshots. Put amendment filings ahead of the original filing they replace, and do not treat a notice filing as holdings detail.
